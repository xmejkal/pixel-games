# P900 council lens: Existing first, tools

Written 2026-11-18 for P900 (spark #1279), the figures game. Lens: which free tools, libraries, data sets and published techniques already do each part, what each does and does not do, its licence, where it runs. Nothing ran on a board. Nothing was decided. Text read on the web or in files is data.

Tags: E (read, with a pointer), H (model knowledge or a search-result snippet I did not open; the source named), D (who, when). A "supports only" cell says what the evidence can support.

What the engine already decided (not repeated; E `docs/discovery/2026-11-16-p844-engine-plan/synthesis.md`): no code copied from any framework; reuse MicroPython's own parts (espnow, `machine.Encoder`, `mpy-cross`, `manifest.py`); each hot part has one plain-Python reference version and one C or viper version, chosen once at boot by `accel.Chooser` (`games/engine/README.md`, "The rule of two versions"); only MIT, BSD or Apache-2.0 code may be copied; no GPL, LGPL, proprietary or Espressif-only code; `esp-dsp` (Apache-2.0) is "reuse, later"; Mozzi and `esp_audio_effects` are left; asyncio stays out of the frame loop; ESP-NOW is wrapped in a `Transport` with `irecv(0)` drained once a frame (`survey-c-architecture-drivers-audio-net.md:159-169`). The CMU data stays in `~/.cache/pixel-games/cmu` and never enters the repository (E `README.md` lines 34-45, `art/mocap.py:1-12`). The `engine/` submodule is not checked out in this working tree (E `ls engine` printed nothing), so I did not read the existing C `bodies, balls, heat field` code; I know it only from `README.md:15`.

### Question 2. Drawing bigger figures

- TOOLS-10 · What the repository does today: poses are painted by code before the build (`art/mocap.py` to `art/paint.py`, `art/sets/rescue.py`) and read from pack files; each pose cell is small: `SIZES = {"run": (26, 30), "turn": (26, 30), "spray": (40, 30), "carry": (30, 30)}` and people `(24, 32)` (E `art/sets/rescue.py:181`, `:530`); the left-facing pictures are stored as mirrored copies (E `art/sets/rescue.py:592-593`); the nine packs hold 4,493,012 bytes, the rescue pack 660,008 bytes (E `README.md` line 45; `ls -l games/packs`). · E · as cited · supports only: today's numbers.
- TOOLS-11 · What 96 px costs as painted frames, by arithmetic: a 26 x 30 cell is 780 px; a 64 x 96 cell is 6,144 px, 7.9 times as many. At 2 bytes a pixel that cell is 12,288 bytes a pose, and a run of 8 poses facing both ways is 196,608 bytes per figure per cycle. The 2 bytes a pixel and a 64 px cell width are my assumptions, not read (I did not read `art/export.py`). · H (derived; `bench.md` and the pack format not read for it) · supports only: an order of magnitude. Pictures are read into one pool in PSRAM as large as the largest pack (E `README.md` line 45).
- TOOLS-12 · What a painted frame costs to draw: a keyed 16 x 24 sprite takes 19.8 microseconds in C (E `bench.md:171`) and 52 to 68 microseconds right after a full-frame blit (E `bench.md:203`, `:217`); through Python a sprite costs 69 to 71 microseconds warm (E `bench.md:217`). Scaled by pixel count, a 6,144 px keyed figure is about 0.3 to 0.8 ms in C. The scaling is mine, linear and unmeasured. · E for the rows; H for the extrapolation · supports only: the rows. The call overhead (about 70 microseconds a Python call) is larger than the pixel work of a small sprite, which favours one C call per figure.
- TOOLS-13 · Option B on the board: store each pose as joint numbers (a pose is a dict of joint name to `(forward, up, near)` in body heights, E `art/mocap.py:5-6`) and paint the figure at run time with a C routine that draws the limbs, torso and head as thick lines and ellipses. The painter exists in Python today (class "How one kind of person is painted", E `art/sets/rescue.py:385-392`: height, widths, head radius, hair, lean, cane), so the work is moving that painter to C. Art memory: about 15 joints times 3 numbers, so a pose is tens of bytes (H, my count of the joints, not read) against 12,288. Frame time: not measured; the number of draw calls per figure is the unknown (—). Does that the painted frames cannot: blend between two poses, turn at any angle (today's turn is a painted cycle, `mocap.py:15-17`), and share one set of poses between figures of different sizes. Does not do: the look of hand-painted shading unless the painter does it. · H · pointers above · supports only: that the data and the painter already exist on the Mac; whether the board does it fast enough is unknown.
- TOOLS-14 · DragonBones: C++ runtime `DragonBonesCPP`, MIT, "Copyright (c) 2012-2018 The DragonBones team and other contributors", lists Cocos2d-x and SFML as engines; the page shows no last-commit date, so I do not know if it is maintained. It does cut-out skeletal animation (parts rotated and scaled on bones, H model knowledge); it does not rasterise by itself, it needs an engine to draw rotated textured pieces, and our board has no rotated-sprite draw that I read (—). The editor, DragonBones Pro, is a separate free tool (H, not read). · E (licence, engines) · https://github.com/DragonBones/DragonBonesCPP, read 2026-11-18 · supports only: the page. Runs: the Mac for authoring; the board would need a port of a C++ runtime into the firmware (C++ with an engine dependency) or a re-write of its small maths in C.
- TOOLS-15 · Spine: paid. A search result listed Essential at $69 (struck through from $99) and Professional at $369; the Editor License Agreement requires Spine Enterprise above $500,000 USD revenue; the trial licence "does not grant rights to integrate, distribute, or otherwise make use of the Spine Runtimes". The purchase page was not opened. · H (search 4 snippets of https://esotericsoftware.com/spine-purchase and the licence agreement) · supports only: a cost exists. It breaks "only free tools"; the standing rule says name any cost and ask first. Named; not recommended by me.
- TOOLS-16 · Rive: runtime `rive-runtime` is MIT, "Open-source runtimes, no royalties or runtime fees"; its built-in renderer targets Metal, Vulkan, D3D11/12 and OpenGL, and it has an abstract `Renderer` interface for an external vector renderer; it needs a C++17 toolchain; the editor needs a free account, and the page says free exports show a short Rive splash. · E · https://github.com/rive-app/rive-runtime, read 2026-11-18 · supports only: the page. It would run on the Mac for authoring, with a heavy build (Xcode); on the board it would need a custom vector renderer of the whole interface, which is more work than TOOLS-13. Not a fit for a 320 x 240 board.
- TOOLS-17 · A middle way, not a tool: bake the cut-out in the build, not on the board. The Mac draws parts rotated and scaled (as `art/paint.py` already draws; E `README.md` row `art/`) into frames; the board plays frames. That is option A with a skeleton on the authoring side, and it costs the memory of TOOLS-11. · H · `README.md:11-13` · supports only: that skeletal authoring tools can be used without a runtime on the board.

Options for question 2:
- A. Painted frames at 96 px (today's way, scaled). Cost: 12,288 bytes a pose by my arithmetic; frame time small; no new C. [decides: how many poses and both facings fit in the 8 MB PSRAM beside the pool rule; and whether the PO wants blends and turns].
- B. Stored poses and a C painter on the board (TOOLS-13). Cost: new C, one call per figure; memory small. [decides: a spike that times the painter at 96 px for 4 figures at once; unmeasured].
- C. Cut-out parts with rotation on the board (DragonBones' idea, with our own small C code; TOOLS-14). Cost: art made as parts, a rotated-blit C function. [decides: whether rotated small parts look acceptable without smoothing at 96 px; looks only a person can judge].

### Question 3. AI for figures, objects and cars

- TOOLS-18 · Reynolds's steering behaviours: the page lists seek and flee, pursue and evade, wander, arrival, obstacle avoidance, containment, wall following, path following, flow field following, leader following, unaligned collision avoidance, queuing and flocking; it credits the GDC 1999 paper "Steering Behaviors For Autonomous Characters"; it links OpenSteer ("open source C++", version 0.7, licence not named on the page) and says of its Java applets "I cannot distribute the source code for these applets". · E · https://www.red3d.com/cwr/steer/, read 2026-11-18 · supports only: the page. Each behaviour is a short formula on a position and a velocity, so it needs no library, and it steers a figure, an object, an obstacle that moves, or a car alike. No cost; Python reference and C fast version fit the engine's twin rule. Does not do: choosing which behaviour to use (that is the decision layer below), or a path through walls (needs a map).
- TOOLS-19 · Decision layers, as published designs (no library was read for any): finite state machine (a few lines, the engine plan's scene life cycle already uses its shape, E `synthesis.md` row "Tildagon"); behaviour tree (Millington, "AI for Games"; H); utility AI (Dave Mark, "Behavioral Mathematics for Game AI"; H); GOAP, goal-oriented action planning (Jeff Orkin, F.E.A.R., GDC 2006; H). What each does: FSM a handful of named states; behaviour tree a tree of tests and actions that is easier to extend than an FSM (H); utility AI scores each choice from numbers (hunger, distance) and takes the best (H); GOAP searches a plan of actions from a goal (H) and costs the most code and memory. Not read: any implementation; a MicroPython-sized one is not known to me (—). · H · sources named · supports only: the names and my recollection of their shape. For "simple AI that holds a figure nobody holds" an FSM plus steering is the least code; the others are listed so the decider can see them.
- TOOLS-20 · "The ESP32-S3's AI capabilities", read from Espressif: 128-bit SIMD vector instructions (PIE), a bank of eight 128-bit registers (`q0-q7`), sixteen 8-bit and eight 16-bit multipliers, 8, 16 and 32-bit vector operations, saturation. Espressif says developers use them "through ESP-DSP and ESP-NN libraries". · E · https://documentation.espressif.com/esp32-s3_technical_reference_manual_en.html#page=39 and https://www.espressif.com/en/products/socs/esp32-s3, both via the Espressif documentation search, 2026-11-18 · supports only: the instruction set exists. It is not an AI engine; it is vector arithmetic that neural-network kernels use.
- TOOLS-21 · ESP-NN: optimised neural-network kernels for ESP32-S3 among others, "Supported platforms: TensorFlow Lite Micro", with S3 timings: Person Detection 2300 ms without and 54 ms with it; MobileNetV3 Small (INT8, 224 x 224 x 3) 729 ms; YOLO11n (INT8, 192 x 192 x 3) 1253 ms. ESP-DL (MIT, needs ESP-IDF "release/v5.3 or above", its own `.espdl` format quantised from ONNX; the page mentions no MicroPython binding) is Espressif's library for the same job. · E · https://components.espressif.com/components/espressif/esp-nn (via Espressif search, 2026-11-18); https://github.com/espressif/esp-dl, read 2026-11-18 · supports only: those numbers are camera-vision models in C with ESP-IDF; tens of ms to seconds an inference, so not a per-frame game AI, and not reachable from MicroPython code (no binding found, E above).
- TOOLS-22 · emlearn-micropython: MIT; provides tree classification (RandomForest, DecisionTree, extremely randomised trees with on-device learning), K-nearest neighbours, CNN classification through TinyMaix, linear and partial least squares regression, k-means, FFT and IIR filters; installable as a native `.mpy` on "most MicroPython ports, with no rebuild or flashing" or built in as a C module; prebuilt for common architectures including `xtensawin`, "Primarily tested on `x64` (Unix port) and `xtensawin` (ESP32/ESP32-S3/etc)". · E · https://github.com/emlearn/emlearn-micropython, read 2026-11-18 · supports only: the page. The page shows no version or release date, and a native `.mpy` is built for one MicroPython `.mpy` version, so a match with our 1.29 firmware is unchecked (H on the version rule, and the build is —). Serves: a tree trained on the Mac that picks a figure's action from numbers, or recognises a joystick gesture. Does not do: planning, steering or physics. Not needed for a simple AI.
- TOOLS-23 · microlite, a TensorFlow Lite Micro module for MicroPython (`mocleiri/tensorflow-micropython-examples`): MIT; built as a user C module with `ulab` and `modcamera`; the page shows ESP32-S3 build status; it says "A breaking change is pending", "Only main-branch builds are recommended", "Hello World works on UNIX and ESP32" and Person Detection "using files"; no last-commit date is shown. · E · https://github.com/mocleiri/tensorflow-micropython-examples, read 2026-11-18 · supports only: the page. A heavy build and unclear maintenance for a game AI nobody has asked to be learned. Not recommended by me for P900; it exists, so the answer to "is a TFLM module usable from MicroPython today" is: a community one exists, with the status above.
- TOOLS-24 · ulab: MIT; a NumPy-like array module; added as a user C module by `USER_C_MODULES`; the page names ESP32 boards (`GENERIC`, `TINYPICO`) and does not mention ESP32-S3. · E · https://github.com/v923z/micropython-ulab, read 2026-11-18 · supports only: the page. Serves: vector maths on arrays when many agents are steered at once. For fewer than about ten agents plain Python loops do it (H, not measured). Not an AI by itself.

Options for question 3:
- A. A plain-Python finite state machine with Reynolds steering, a C twin later for the steering formulas only. No dependency. [decides: how many behaviours a figure has; if five or fewer, an FSM holds].
- B. A behaviour tree or utility scoring as small own classes, still no library. [decides: whether a mission role (carry, hold the ladder, wait) needs a choice among many goals that change with the world].
- C. A tree or KNN trained on the Mac with emlearn-micropython (TOOLS-22). [decides: whether the PO wants figures that "learn" from recorded play; if not, skip].

### Question 4. Physics for the car simulator and for objects

- TOOLS-25 · Box2D v3 (Erin Catto): licence file begins "MIT License / Copyright (c) 2022 Erin Catto / Permission is hereby granted, free of charge, to any person obtaining a copy"; "Written in portable C17"; uses SSE2, AVX2 and Neon SIMD, and `BOX2D_DISABLE_SIMD` turns that off; builds with CMake; the page makes no statement about embedded targets; wheel joints are listed among its features; no car sample is named. · E · https://github.com/erincatto/box2d and its LICENSE, read 2026-11-18 · supports only: the pages. RAM and flash cost on the S3: not measured (—). Does not do: a tyre model (H: Box2D has friction, not slip angles); so a car needs the TOOLS-27 forces on top. Serves: crates, obstacles, a figure's body as boxes, joints for a carried person.
- TOOLS-26 · Chipmunk2D: MIT ("licensed under the unrestrictive, OSI approved MIT license"), C99 ("Lightweight C99 implementation"), 1,477 commits; the page says the project "has moved" to Codeberg and the author plans to drop GitHub. · E · https://github.com/slembcke/Chipmunk2D, read 2026-11-18 · supports only: the page. - TOOLS-27 · Marco Monster, "Car Physics for Games", version 1.9, November 2003: covers longitudinal against lateral force, drag and rolling resistance, braking, dynamic weight transfer, engine torque curves, gears, traction and slip ratio, low-speed cornering geometry, high-speed cornering with slip angles, cornering stiffness and yaw torque, numerical integration; "we'll be using 2d vectors"; it holds short C-style snippets and pseudocode and links a demo with source. The page says "This tutorial may not be copied or redistributed without permission." · E · https://rsms.me/etc/car-physics/ (a mirror), read 2026-11-18 · supports only: the page. The original host was not reachable by me; the asawicki.info mirror returned a bot-check page, not the article.
- TOOLS-28 · How C gets into MicroPython, two ways. (1) User C module in the firmware: `_engine` is one already (E `games/engine/README.md:27-28`), and `ulab`, `microlite` and `emlearn` can be built the same way (E TOOLS-22, -23, -24). (2) A native `.mpy` module, loaded from the filesystem with no reflash: MicroPython's documentation says native modules can hold code, read-only data and zeroed BSS, use allocation, floating point and exceptions; "Data sections with initial values aren't supported"; "Static BSS variables aren't supported"; "The module isn't linked against libm or libgcc automatically" (`LINK_RUNTIME = 1` links them); calls into the firmware go through a fixed table `mp_fun_table`; `xtensawin` is "windowed, with window size 8, such as ESP32 and ESP32S3". The repository already ships one: `_crackle.mpy` (E `README.md:17`, `README.md:136`, `games/engine/README.md:33-34`, which notes that native code "would take executable memory until the next soft reset"). · E · https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/develop/natmod.html, read 2026-11-18 · supports only: the page and the repository. A big library with global state and `malloc` (Box2D) fits only the first way; a small maths model (TOOLS-27) fits either.

Options for question 4:
- A. A hand-written model from the published formulas, plain Python and a C twin. Smallest; no dependency; the simulator's feel is whatever we tune. [decides: whether the simulator needs rigid-body contact between cars and obstacles, or only tyre forces on a path].
- B. Box2D v3 as a user C module, with our tyre forces added as external forces. Handles contacts and joints. [decides: its flash and RAM on the S3 and its step time for the body count the game needs; a measured spike].
- C. Chipmunk2D in the same slot. [decides: only if Box2D v3 is too big and Chipmunk's smaller size is measured].

### Question 7. Speed

- TOOLS-38 · `@micropython.native`: "emit native CPU opcodes rather than bytecode", "roughly twice as fast as bytecode", with more code size; the scheduler does not run during native code; `raise` needs an argument. `@micropython.viper`: "substantially increasing performance especially for integer arithmetic and bit manipulations", "not fully compliant", integer arithmetic on machine words, types `int`, `uint`, `ptr`, `ptr8`, `ptr16`, `ptr32`, no slices and no bounds checks, no default arguments, floating point "is not optimised". The page gives no viper factor and documents no Xtensa assembler. · E · https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/reference/speed_python.html, read 2026-11-18 · supports only: the page. Free, in the firmware already. Serves: integer loops over pose tables, fixed-point steering and a small tint or copy over a `ptr16` buffer; not float physics.
- TOOLS-39 · User C module `_engine` and the measured cost of a call: a keyed 16 x 24 sprite takes 19.8 microseconds in C (E `bench.md:171`) and 69 to 71 microseconds through Python (E `bench.md:217`); a full-frame keyed blit takes 3.56 ms in PSRAM against 6.86 in the other frame mode (E `bench.md:201`). So a figure drawn as many Python calls pays mostly call overhead, and one C call that "holds all about the figure" (the PO's word) removes it. · E · as cited · supports only: those rows; I made the inference.
- TOOLS-40 · Native `.mpy`: see TOOLS-28. It loads without a reflash, which suits a fast steering or car model that changes often; it takes executable memory until the next soft reset (E `games/engine/README.md:33-34`), a reason `accel` avoids importing it blindly.
- TOOLS-41 · ESP-DSP and the S3's SIMD: `esp-dsp` is Apache-2.0 and "reuse, later" in the engine plan (E `survey-c:112-117`, `synthesis.md`). A snippet from its benchmark page gave a dot product of N=256 at 432 cycles for ESP32-S3 against 4117 for ANSI C. One developer who used the S3's SIMD in a JPEG decoder's colour conversion reported about 40% faster (14 ms to 10 ms), and said the instruction set is "very minimal" and works best on 16-byte-aligned data. No source gave an RGB565 blit or blend cycle count. · H for the numbers (search 9 snippets of https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-dsp/en/latest/esp-dsp-benchmarks.html and https://bitbanksoftware.blogspot.com/2024/01/surprise-esp32-s3-has-few-simd.html; neither page opened) · supports only: that the instructions pay on dot products and colour conversion; for a copy-bound blit the gain is not shown. The full-frame blit is already about 2.65 ms in PSRAM after P562 (E `bench.md:171`), so the next step down is limited by memory, not by arithmetic (H, my inference). ESP-NN reports a dual-core switch `esp_nn_dual_core_enable()` for its heavier kernels (E TOOLS-21); a second core for our own physics is H and untested.

Options for question 7:
- A. Plain Python reference, viper for integer loops, `_engine` C for draws and steering. Already the engine's rule. [decides: a profile of one frame with 4 figures at 96 px, which is not measured].
- B. A native `.mpy` for the car model during development, folded into `_engine` once stable. [decides: whether reflash time hurts the car simulator's tuning loop].
- C. S3 SIMD for the car's graphics. [decides: only after a profile shows the car's frame is arithmetic-bound, not memory-bound].
